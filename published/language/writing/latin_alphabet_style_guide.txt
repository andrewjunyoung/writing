           Young's Definitive Style Guide for the Latin Alphabet


Status of This Memo

   This memo defines a style standard for text written in the Latin
   alphabet. It is a draft, and is incomplete in places. Distribution
   of this memo is unlimited.

Abstract

   This document specifies conventions for capitalisation, acronyms,
   punctuation, custom notation, and numerals in text written in the
   [extended] Latin alphabet.

1.  Introduction

   This document sets out a single, consistent style for writing in the
   Latin alphabet. Each section gives one or more standards, followed by
   examples and, where available, an explanation of the reasoning.

1.1.  Requirements Language

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", and "MAY"
   in this document are to be interpreted as described in RFC 2119.

1.2.  Notation Used in This Document

   Examples are marked as follows:

      Example:    A conforming example.
      Not:        A non-conforming example ...
      Instead:    ... and its conforming replacement.
      Also:       An equally acceptable alternative.


2.  Upper Case

2.1.  Sentence Heads and Tails

   Standard 2.1.1: Sentences MUST begin with a single capital letter. A
   capital letter is defined to be «the upper case form of a single
   character from the [extended] Latin alphabet».

      Example:  The quick brown fox jumped over the lazy dog.

      Not:      LLamas are an animal native to South America.
      Instead:  Llamas are an animal native to South America.

      Not:      IJsselmeer is the name of the closed off inland bay in
                the central Netherlands.
      Instead:  Ijsselmeer is the name of the closed off inland bay in
                the central Netherlands.

   Standard 2.1.2: All characters in a sentence other than the first
   character MUST be lower case, unless covered by one of the exceptions
   in this document.

   Explanation:

      This follows a common convention among all dual case languages to
      capitalise the first characters of sentences. We accept this
      convention, for it helps guide the eye towards the beginnings of
      sentences and passages.

      All other letters should be lower case, firstly because they are
      more readable, and secondly because this makes the text thereafter
      more predictable, including for searching text.

      We restrict the use of digraphs because digraphs are language
      specific and inconsistent. Some languages treat digraphs as 2
      characters for collation, while others treat the same digraph as
      1 character. In loanwords, readers should not be expected to know
      rules for digraphs. It is most consistent to treat the Latin
      alphabet as having exactly 26 letters, and that these are always
      separate.

2.2.  Proper Nouns

   Standard 2.2.1: Proper nouns MUST be capitalised in all contexts, but
   only when used as a noun. Words which take their root from a proper
   noun MUST NOT be capitalised. Proper nouns normally include «the»
   where appropriate.

      Example:  I just called Mary Goldberg to let her know that im
                home.

      Example:  Did you know that she studied at Cambridge University?

      Not:      I thought she studied at the University of Oxford?
      Instead:  I thought she studied at The University Of Oxford?

      Not:      California is a state in the USA.
      Instead:  California is a state in The USA.

      Not:      I believe in god.
      Instead:  I believe in God.

      Not:      I believe in a God.
      Instead:  I believe in a god.

      Not:      Did you know that they're German?
      Instead:  Did you know that they're german?


3.  Acronyms

   Standard 3.1: Acronyms, including initialisms, MUST be written as
   consecutive upper case letters with no spaces or delimiters between
   them.

      Example:  The US is a signatory country to NATO.

      Example:  As of 2018, the permanent UN security council members
                are China, France, Russia, The UK, and The USA.

   Standard 3.2: Acronyms which are not initialisms MAY be written in
   lower case letters. This SHOULD be kept consistent with the rules on
   proper nouns (Section 2.2).

      Not:      The US is a signatory country to nato.
      Instead:  The US is a signatory country to NATO.
      Also:     The US is a signatory country to Nato.

      Example:  UNICEF is a subsidiary of the UN.
      Also:     Unicef is a subsidiary of the UN.


4.  Punctuation

4.1.  Colons

   Standard 4.1.1: Colons connect related, otherwise incomplete
   sentences.

   Standard 4.1.2: Colons describe preceding sentences, including with
   further deductive or descriptive statements.

   Standard 4.1.3: Colons introduce dialog, both in plays and in
   written passages.

   Standard 4.1.4: Colons indicate omission of text, including letters,
   words, or sections (see also Section 4.7).

      Example:  Kings Cross S:t Pancras

      Example:  D:r Cruz runs an office on the edge of town.

      Example:  The : fox jumps :.

   Standard 4.1.5: Colons are used in enumerated lists to separate
   numbers and the following text.

      Example:  We need to

                   1: find the bag;
                   2: get the money;
                   3: be home in time for dinner.

4.2.  Dashes

   Standard 4.2.1: Dashes denote ranges of time and space.

      Example:  The French and Indian War (1754 – 1763) was fought in
                western Pennsylvania and along the present US – Canada
                border.

      Example:  The Tokyo – Osaka express train departs at 3.15 PM.

   Standard 4.2.2: Dashes denote relation in certain contexts.

      Example:  The US – Canada border spans east – west.

      Example:  The Eagles beat the Hawks 31 – 0.

   Standard 4.2.3: Dashes denote subcategorisation, including in dates
   and locations. When used with numbers, the dash MUST be written
   without surrounding spaces.

      Example:  The date is 2018–11–3.

      Example:  He addressed the package to «China – Beijing –
                Wangfujing».

      Example:  Welcome to the Computing 3–12 course, where we cover
                advanced computer architecture.

      Example:  Answer question 1–a first.

   Standard 4.2.4: All dashes MUST be en dashes (–).

4.3.  Semicolons

   Standard 4.3.1: Semicolons are used for iteration, and in place of
   iterative commas.

      Example:  The people present were Jamie, a man from New Zealand;
                John, the milkman's son; and George, a gaunt kind of
                man with no friends.

      Example:  Several fast food restaurants can be found within the
                following cities: London, England; Paris, France;
                Dublin, Ireland; Madrid, Spain.

      Not:      Here are three examples of familiar sequences: one,
                two, three; a, b, c; first, second, third.
      Instead:  Here are three examples of familiar sequences: «one;
                two; three»; «a; b; c»; «first; second; third».

4.4.  Question Marks

   Standard 4.4.1: Opening and closing question marks (¿ ?) MUST be
   used to enclose question sentences.

      Example:  She came to me and asked: «¿What time is it?».

      Example:  Čingis Xan (¿1162? – 1227) was the founder of the
                Mongol Empire.

   Standard 4.4.2: Opening and closing question marks follow the same
   rules as opening and closing brackets, respectively.

   Standard 4.4.3: Question marks MAY be used one after another in
   conjunction.

      Example:  ¿Is it written in good form? ¿Style? ¿Meaning?

   Standard 4.4.4: Question marks take the place of periods when used
   at the end of sentences.

4.5.  Exclamation Marks

   Standard 4.5.1: Exclamation marks (¡ !) follow the same rules as
   question marks (Section 4.4).

      Example:  «¡Stop!» yelled the guard.

4.6.  Commas

   Standard 4.6.1: Commas are principally used for the separation of
   clauses. A comma appears at the end of a phrase, directly after the
   final word of the phrase, and is followed by a single space.

   Standard 4.6.2: When clauses are separated with conjunctions, the
   comma MUST appear before the conjunction.

      Example:  Mary walked to the party, but she couldnt walk home.

      Example:  Designer clothes are silly, and i can't afford them
                anyway.

      Example:  ¡Don't push that button, or twelve tons of high
                explosives will go off right under our feet!

   Standard 4.6.3: Commas are advised, but OPTIONAL, after sentence
   initiating adverbs such as «however»; «in fact»; and
   «nevertheless».

      Example:  Therefore, a comma would be appropriate in this
                sentence.
      Also:     Therefore a comma would be appropriate in this
                sentence.

      Example:  Nevertheless, it is not needed.
      Also:     Nevertheless it is not needed.

   Standard 4.6.4: When adverbs are used mid phrase, commas SHOULD be
   used.

      Example:  In this sentence, furthermore, commas would be called
                for.

      Not:      This sentence is similar; however, a semicolon is used
                as well.
      Instead:  This sentence is similar. However, a semicolon is used
                as well.

   Standard 4.6.5: Geographical names MUST NOT include commas.

      Example:  Take this package to Tallahassee Florida.

   Standard 4.6.6: Dates MUST NOT include commas.

      Example:  February 3rd 1922: on that day I discovered the meaning
                of loss.

   Standard 4.6.7: The preferred notation for parenthetical phrases is
   the bracket (Section 4.12).

4.7.  Ellipses

   Standard 4.7.1: Ellipses MUST be written as 2 unspaced periods (..).

   Standard 4.7.2: Ellipses indicate omission of text, including
   letters, words, or sections.

   Standard 4.7.3: Ellipses indicate silence.

   Standard 4.7.4: Ellipses MAY denote ranges of time and space (see
   also Section 4.2).

      Example:  The French and Indian War (1754 .. 1763) was fought in
                western Pennsylvania and along the present US – Canada
                border.

      Example:  The Tokyo .. Osaka express train departs at 3.15 PM.

4.8.  Periods

   Standard 4.8.1: Periods appear at the end of sentences, directly
   after the final word of the sentence, and are followed by a single
   space. The character immediately after a period SHOULD be a capital
   letter.

4.9.  Quotation Marks

   Standard 4.9.1: By default, double angle quotes («..») SHOULD be
   used to indicate segments of text with a common purpose. This
   includes speech, thoughts, and quotations.

      Example:  «Come closer.» he said softly.

      Example:  Hamlet: «To be or not to be, that is the question.»

      Example:  At the time i thought «Gosh, that's a nice dress.».

      Example:  In the words of Jean-Paul Sartre: «Man is condemned to
                be free; because once thrown into the world, he is
                responsible for everything he does.».

4.10.  Nested Quotation Marks

   Standard 4.10.1: Nested quotation marks MUST NOT differ from the
   outermost quotation marks. Where clarity is warranted, the level of
   nesting MAY be indicated by a subscript number.

      Example:  My sister says «The man told me «You need to leave
                now»».

      Example:  My sister says «₁The man told me «₂You need to leave
                now»₂»₁.

4.11.  Slashes

   Standard 4.11.1: Slashes indicate alternative choices.

      Example:  ¿Do you agree Y / N?

   Standard 4.11.2: Slashes indicate choices in the case of ambiguity,
   particularly when this affects word choice. In some cases, round
   brackets may be favoured (see Section 4.12).

      Example:  The waiter / waitress hasnt come yet.

      Example:  The designation Assyrian / Chaldean / Syriac appeared on
                american and swedish censuses in response to the Syriac
                naming dispute.

      Example:  If he / she wants to come, then that is completely
                fine.

4.12.  Brackets

   Standard 4.12.1: Round brackets MAY be used to indicate choices in
   the case of ambiguity, particularly when this affects word choice
   (see also Section 4.11).

      Example:  If (s)he wants to come, then that is completely fine.

      Example:  The culprit(s) are still on the run.

   Standard 4.12.2: Asides are indicated by square brackets ([ ]).

      Example:  If the dog falls ill [ which I doubt will happen ] then
                we can take it to the vet.

      Example:  If I slip on ice [ funny though that would be ] its
                likely to hurt.

      Example:  The lion [ the biggest of the cats ] [ the king of the
                jungle ] sat in its enclosure unstirred by the
                photographers around it.

      Example:  [ Written on a list ] «Buy eggs [ done ].»

4.13.  Defunct Punctuation

   The following punctuation is NOT RECOMMENDED for common use. It is
   listed alphabetically so that readers may look these characters up.
   Use of any of them is considered bad practice.

      1: em dash (—)
      2: hyphen (except as a letter of the Latin alphabet)
      3: hyphen-minus (-)
      4: quotation marks (" " “ ” ' ' ‘ ’)


5.  Custom Notation

   Standard 5.1: Custom notation MAY be introduced, such as in plays,
   using the backslash and curly brackets to indicate that text is to be
   considered a certain way. Custom notation MUST be defined either
   before or alongside its first appearance within a text.

      Example:  Hamlet: \aside{A little more than kin, and less than
                kind.}


6.  Numerals

   Standard 6.1: Numbers in enumerated lists MUST be followed by a
   colon (see Standard 4.1.5).
