We began our trip in Sapporo, having flown in from Hongkong. On the way from the airport, we noticed a few small families, children with parents, and some small Swiss-style mountains stocked by somewhat uglier utilitarian Japanese homes 1 or 2 stories tall.
These flatter homes quickly transformed into taller box buildings around 10 floors tall, which indicated to us that we had arrived in the Sapporo city limits.

Sapporo is a fairly small city. The main station is lain out much like the stations in Tokyo, with the same interior decor and styling that you would expect - maps, mechanical machines for buying train tickets, and some staff to guide you. This was the central station for the whole town, and it was fairly crowded, with maybe an average of 3-6 feet between you and any other person. This made it hard to travel with bags.

From here we went to our hotel which was around 2 subway stops from the main station. We could have walked, but this would have been unpleasant since we had many bags.

Near the hotel was a small 7/11 convenience store across the street, some ads with blonde-haired foreign models (as seem to be popular in Hokkaido), some small gyoza restaurants, bars, and a small tourist attraction (I believe it was a theater) set up by foreign missionaries when they were in Hokkaido.

The theater was somewhat American style - it was made of lightweight wood, had a small belltower, and western style ornamentation. The only indication that it had been appropriated to Japan was a calligraphic sign bearing the building's name in Japanese.

If you were to walk a few blocks over, you would eventually reach the main road ("oodoori") where you would see some pedestrian overpasses, a handful of protestant churches, and further on a street-style shopping mall with coffee shops, tourist shops, and restaurants, as are popular in Japan more generally.

On our first night we went for sea urchin ("uni") and had a bowl. The restaurant served unlimited beer with any meal. I suspect they had not encountered many young western patrons who could easily have made back their $30 sea urchin bowl on this deal. Out of politeness I had only 2 Sapporo beers, but the food was overall excellent. This was one of my favorite meals in Hokkaido.

As is often true in Japan, the tastiest food can be located anywhere, and in this case this restaurant was a short turn off the main red light entertainment district in Japan, where you could see advertisements for the kinds of girls that would spend their time with you and entertain you for the night. While prostitution is not legal in Japan, anything less than it goes, and girls that will simply provide you with basic friendly accompaniment are very common fare.

During our few-days stay in the city, I made a few walks around town. It was a 30 minute walk from our hotel to the river. We were visiting in summer, and it was apparent that the stream's winter snow melt had concluded and the river had been reduced to a trickle. The infrastructure to manage the river vastly exceeded the river flow at that time of the year, and the wider river park could be described as a combination of flood control that doubled up as a summer seasonal park, or so it seemed.

The river park had bike paths, some basic trees, many reeds, and some stones and engineering work to manage and berak the flow of the river's erosion.

I patronized several coffee shops in the area. In my previous trip to Hokkaido in 2016, I did not notice much coffee presence. This was not my experience this time in 2025. Coffee shops had become ubiquitous, but mostly did not offer the same milk and frothing options that American coffee shops do. For the most part, your options were americano, or café au lait. I was not impressed with the coffee in Hokkaido. I tried it in many locations, and the best coffee I had in Hokkaido would have been the worst coffee I could find in New York or even San Francisco. Their coffee was much to bitter, but not in the way of rich bitter flavor.

If you walked around town for enough time, you would notice Hokkaido's "substantial" foreign population, often with noticeably different hygiene habits, manners, and music taste to the local Japanese. This was especially noticeable near to the river, and it seemed to me that perhaps 1-5% of Sapporo's summertime population was non-Japanese. There were many tourists, mostly Chinese, who stood out as tourists and not as long-term residents.

Sapporo's overall culture is much the same as it was in 2016 - much more globalized, tourist-focused, and less Japanese-oriented than many of Japan's other cities. The shops and restaurants you would see cater well to Sapporo's foreign tourist population, serve the foods that they would desire such as Sushi, sell them the Japanese brands they would wish to buy, and appeal to their entertainment sensibilities.

The shopping mall catered much more to Japanese entertainment sensibilities with bars, izakaya restaurants, and ramen. The demographics of each area would agree with my assessment.

Although I did not enjoy much Japanese coffee, Sapporo had a huge number of cafés open during morning hours. Mostly they would serve good food rather than good coffee, and seemed like the ideal spot for local ladies to meet and chat over a cake, biscuits, and so on.

Sapporo is a fairly small town, and within 30 minutes you can find yourself in firmly suburban areas, where cars go fast, people like to ride pickup trucks for their small town blue collar jobs, and buildings are suitable for family life.

I found these districts to be very American. The housing style was often wood with front lawns, grass, trees, and an esthetic enjoyment for nature. Some regions of these outlying town would resort to the more Japanese-style brick and concrete and forego grass, but in Sapporo there was often a mix. You would be able to find civic buildings like libraries interlain with the houses. This was yet another indication of a more American planning style.

In these smaller exurbs to Hokkaido, you would experience the destination shopping centers such as Daikanyama libraries and big-box stores. These places could be stayed at for multiple hours. In terms of book variety, Daikanyama seems to be losing some variety and converging on the western Barnes & Noble's stocking habits. You can find all the secular western canon like Tolstoy, Voltaire, and Steinbeck, and Japan's modern authors like KAWABATA Yasunari and MURAKAMI Haruki. And you could find your unfamiliar authors writing on topics like modern philosophy, pluralism, multiculturalism, demographic change, history (as seen from a post-1945 liberal viewpoint). But you cannot find books which contradict this worldview.

You could find several books on Buddhism, many on meditation in a general sense, and a couple on mainline protestant Christianity. But you would be unable to find any book which persuades you to be Christian, or Muslim, or explains these viewpoints. You can find books about "why China is on the rise", and maybe even a book that explains the mainstream Chinese world view, but you would not be able to find a book which teaches you about the details of their economy.

I find this to be disappointing. I came in looking for "the kind of books you can't read in English". I feel that the information gap between English literature and Japanese literature is shrinking, and the ideas that Japanese authors are putting onto paper to be converging with what Americans like to publish. I purchased 3 Classical Chinese reference books and left. These 3 reference books are designed to prepare you for university exam questions, and give you some grammatical practice, but I could not find any books which would actually give you any Classical Chinese text to read. Again, I found this to be disappointing.

The shifting literary environment in Japan is one I have been disappointed with in the past. At one time in Nagoya in 2016, I found a book of Classical Chinese poetry. Imagine a rare find like this in an English book store: a whole 3-inch thick book of Latin poetry! It would be a rare gem! I wanted to buy this book but being 18 and without money I had to forego it. I have never found a book like it ever since.

These kind of books seemed to be more common in Japan in 2016, and relatively much less common in the bookstores I visited in 2025. I cannot explain why. Perhaps, like America, the literary elites do not wish to cater to a certain type of politics that Japan undoubtedly has.

Comparing this with the conversations I had in Japan, I can firmly say that there are unwritten opinions that you can hear and openly discuss in bars, cars, and restaurants, which don't make it to the shelves of libraries.

Outside of Sapporo, we continued on our way. Up until the Hidaka mountains which run through central Japan, you could see persistent signs of international tourism, such as ski resorts. You could visit lighthouses, go to spas and old resort towns which are relics of the era before interantional plane travel, which sell hot springs. This part of Japan was geologically very interesting, but socially unremarkable. Kids were somewhat rare, especially for a rural area; towns are small and revolve around tourism or farming.

At one time we drove to the northwestern tip of Hokkaido, near to Russia. The geology was excellent. The cliffs were sheer rock faces, and while the climate near the sea was humid and hot, you could drive up the mountains and find a mountaintop prairie. It was like another world. The land was flat enough to farm, despite a steep ascent to get up. If a farmer were willing to haul supplies up from the world below, they could easily graze sheep or cows on the grass, or farm crops. If you could get a tractor up to the top of the mountain, it is likely you'd be able to use it in the mountaintop prairies. I say "prairie" as a translation of the Japanese word "sougen", literally "grass plains/prairies". Perhaps "grasslands" would be a better word, but prairie captures the spirit.

In this part of Japan there were stratovolcanoes stretching far up into and above the clouds, black sand beaches, and sea stacks. The landscape was somewhat similar to the Faroe Islands. For somebody who likes dramatic landscapes, or beaches, the views and nature were incredible.

But, there were almost no people. Almost all restaurants were not open during weekdays. I suspect that these restaurants are owned by somebody who lives in another town, and that they'll open up shop on Friday and weekends when business is enough to make ends meet. Restauranst with "weekend-only" was extremely common in this part of Hokkaido, which left us looking for a place to eat for several hours. When we did find a place to eat, they were very often reservation-only and packed full. I did wonder if this was essentially an anti-tourist policy, but I could not tell. My best guess is that it is not an anti-tourist policy and that it was a sincere reality, but I found no strong evidence in eihter direction. It is true that restaurants were very rarely open.

Moreover, Hokkaido was mostly cloudy. Almost every day, you'd be lucky to have blue skies for longer than a few minutes. Even if you were in the mountains, if the winds were blowing into the mountains, you would find yourself driving through clouds. Over our 7-8 day vacation I don't believe that we had a single day that wasn't fully overcast at some points, although when we were high in the Hidaka mountains we did exchange our fully gray overcast weather for fluffy cumulus clouds in blue skies. I very much liked this part of Hokkaido, since it was rural, bright, hardly touched by man, and pretty.

This is something you would experience when crossing the Hidaka mountains. The best American analogy for the Hidaka mountains is southern Appalachia. Even West Virginia has more human settlement and signs of activity. The Hidaka mountains are covered in thick forest and bugs. There are almost no trails to hike, and the few trails that do exist often exist for the purpose of a tourist attraction or building, and are accessible by car. The kind of thick forest that covers the mountain would be OK for hiking if you had a firm understanding of the local terrain, and a gun for bears, which is almost an impossibility in Japan. Ticks are a serious risk, although lyme disease is not.

A small town barman told me that while lyme disease is not common, russian ticks do bear a different disease which results in death. Cases are rare but increasing due to international tourism. Even so, the rates of lyme disease seem to be below 5% of the tick population, as are the rates of this russian tick disease. Both these rates are far below the rates of lyme disease you see among New England ticks, which are reportedly between 1/3 and 1/2 of the New England tick population.

Once you cross the Hidaka mountains, you enter a flat swampy marshland that stretches for miles. The terrain appears to be perfect for farming, but although the harvest is plentiful the workers are few. The villages in the west of Hokkaido are overwhelmingly old, with a few exceptions, and mostly revolve around either farming, fishing, or tourism, with a few exceptions. Due to a tsunami originating in Kamchatka, we had to cut our visit to Kushiro, which had been locked down for safety reasons. We saw a few army supply trucks leaving Kushiro on our way through western Hokkaido. 

We did find alternative accommodation in the western middle volcanic arc of Hokkaido, at the Prince Hotel at Lake Kussharo. The local town seemed to be a farming and tourist town. The views remained overcast. At some points in the mountain, you could barely see more than a few feet in front of you. The farming and soil quality, including the weather, appeared to be excellent, but yet again the labor supply seemed to be far below the land's capacity for agriculture.

We visited a few towns along the eastern coast of Hokkaido. The first was a fishing village, for which the entire economy seemed to revolve around fisherman. They semeed very unsurprised that we were foreign, and even had English menus at nearly all the restaurants. While I didn't see any other foreigners in my walks or drives around town, I suspect that either foreign laborers or foreign (perhaps Russian) fisherman had been to this town before.

The fish was the freshest we tasted in our whole trip. For $10 equivalent, we ate an entire bowl of fresh sushi of many varieties, including tuna, scallop, squid, and more. My guess is that around half of the cost of the meal was probably the rice they provided with the fish, which is not native to Hokkaido. We did not see any rice paddies in Hokkaido.

Near the fishing town we saw several fishing warehouses. The boat inventory in this part of Japan appeared to be a few years behind the avant-gard of fishing in America, which suggests to me that private equity or banking has not yet discovered or chosen to invest in these towns and their businesses. Although most of the town's economy seemed to be related to fishing, the fishing fleet was not particularly sophisticated. I suspect there's something that one could infer about the local fish habits, or maybe even competition with international fishing regulations and competition. But I could not infer anything from my trip, other than the surprising lack of capital investment into better boats. I have seen individual people in Miami Florida with boats better suited to fishing that what I saw in that fishing town.

The region was very marshy, with a huge inland "lake" that connected to the sea with a small break in the edge of a lake. I believe it was perhaps an infilled volcanic crater. As a visitor, one would notice flat marshy land filled with reeds, bugs, and bug-eaters like spiders and birds, who were no doubt living off the fat of the land.
